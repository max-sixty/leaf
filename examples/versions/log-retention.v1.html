Raw request logs: 400 days down to 45

Raw request logs are 61% of what we store and almost none of what we read. Below is what I would cut, the evidence I cut it on, and the two weeks it takes to land. Quote anything the evidence does not carry and I will go back to it before this becomes a change request.

What changes

Raw request logs expire after 45 days instead of 400. The per-minute aggregates built from them — status class, latency percentiles, route, region — keep the full 400 days and are unaffected, as are audit records, which are a separate store on a separate schedule.

That is 18.4 TB of hot storage returned and about £3,100 a month, against a bill where the raw logs are the single largest line.

Where 45 comes from

45 days is the ninety-fifth percentile of the age of the oldest log line any query touched, over the eighteen months of query history we keep. The distribution is not a curve with a tail; it is a wall. Ninety per cent of reads land inside a week, the rest inside six, and then nothing until the quarterly compliance export, which reads aggregates and not raw lines.

45 rather than 42 is a fortnight's rounding, so a query written on the last day of a six-week investigation still lands.

What it costs us

Two years of incident write-ups name a log older than six weeks exactly twice. Both times the question was answered from the aggregates instead, so the 45 days costs us a search we have not run.

Nothing here is a retention obligation. Legal's requirement is on the audit store, which keeps seven years and is not part of this.

How it lands

  • Week one: the expiry job runs in report-only mode and posts what it would have deleted, per team, to the storage channel.
  • Week one, in parallel: anyone whose dashboards read raw logs directly hears from me, because those are the queries that break rather than slow down.
  • Week two: expiry runs for real, oldest first, at a rate that returns the storage over four days rather than in one drop.

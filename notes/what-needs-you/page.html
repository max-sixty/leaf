design research

What needs you: which unit should Leaf count?

An Ask ends when it's answered. A thread doesn't: it tracks whose move it is, what is still owed, whether it's finished, and whether you need to look at it with only the last speaker, one optional flag and one Resolve button. So you either lose work or keep a list that never empties. The six directions below differ on which unit "needs you" counts. I recommend a loop: one thing one side owes the other, which only one side can act on at a time.

A thread tracks four facts with one flag and one button, and the agent's debt is lost between them

An Ask carries all four facts properly. You owe it, it has a condition for being answered, and meeting that condition closes it. A thread carries them like this:

FactWhat a thread uses todayWhat goes wrong
Whose move is it?The last speaker, plus --awaits on an agent replyAn agent reply without --awaits leaves the thread on nobody, and --awaits goes stale
What is still owed?The workflows answer for the latest user moveResolve settles it: after "please also add a TODO" and Resolve, the agent owes nothing
Is it finished, and who says so?Resolve, by either sideOne gesture means done, dropped, handed off and "later"
Do I need to look at it?Open or resolved, plus unreadAn open thread that owes nothing counts toward no one and never closes itself, so the list can't reach zero

Two transitions cause the pain. "Nobody (still open)" is a state that no count includes and nothing ever closes. "You resolve" from "On the agent" discharges the agent's debt without anyone having paid it.

On real pages, what's missing is results and requests, not questions

A subagent read every thread on the 55 pages in this machine's state home and worktrees that carry real user text (July to September): 169 threads and 224 user messages, each classified by hand.

  • Threads run one way. You ask or instruct, and the agent answers or reports. Decisions already go to Asks, and Asks drain: of 104 on pages you used, 77 were answered and 11 retired, in bursts.
  • Resolve means "off my list", not "done". 73% of resolves come within three minutes of another on the same page, so you sweep. You asked about it outright: "confirm that when I resolve a thread, [it] doesn't discard the work, and can reopen the thread to tell me when the work has finished". Twice a request was resolved within 9 seconds of being sent and never got a reply ("maybe we add a TODO…", "ensure we're clear about this in our CLAUDE.md").
  • The seam between threads and Asks has no rules. 46 threads were opened inside Asks:
    • 15 ask a question before choosing ("what are the tradeoffs?").
    • 10 ask for more material ("show me sketch"), which leaves the Ask waiting on the agent.
    • 13 answer the Ask in prose, and the agent then records the pick by hand ("I've marked the Ask with that choice").
  • A third of your messages mix kinds. For example, "ok, yes, let's have the ring; and add a TODO that it may be a bit too strong". When the agent answered four questions at once, you replied "start with #2 & #3".
  • --awaits and reactions barely work. --awaits is on 5% of agent replies, and only 5 of those 11 end in a question mark. One completion report has sat "On you" for 19 days. There is one real reaction in the whole corpus, and it settles nothing.

Elsewhere, whoever did the work reports it, and the one who asked, or a timer, closes it

Every mature system keeps "whose move", "what's owed" and "is it finished" apart. The human-facing ones add a step the agent-to-agent protocols (A2A, MCP Tasks, FIPA) lack: the requester confirms, cheaply, with a timer or a reply as the backstop. Google's Critique is the deliberate exception: the author resolves reviewer comments on trust.

The best-known attempt at explicit commitment tracking, Winograd and Flores' Coordinator (late 1980s), was rejected by its users, partly because they had to label each message as a request or a promise. Lucy Suchman went further and criticised the accounting itself as "a technology of accountability". Winograd's reply conceded that explicit structure feels like an imposition in unstructured, email-like work. Cohen et al. (2004) point out that the labelling burden "is avoided if messages can be automatically annotated by intent". In Leaf the agent is a language model that already reads every message, so it can do the labelling. Suchman's objection still applies to anything that puts the categories in front of you.
The Coordinator modelled every request as a small state machine; loops keep its shape and drop the labelling

Winograd and Flores built The Coordinator on their "conversation for action": the smallest unit of coordinated work is a conversation between a customer, who asks, and a performer, who does the work. The conversation moves through a fixed set of speech acts, and each act hands the next move to one side.

stateDiagram-v2
  state "Requested" as Requested
  state "Promised" as Promised
  state "Reported done" as Reported
  state "Satisfied" as Satisfied
  state "Closed unsatisfied" as Closed
  [*] --> Requested: customer requests
  Requested --> Promised: performer promises, or customer accepts a counter-offer
  Requested --> Requested: performer counter-offers
  Requested --> Closed: performer declines, or customer withdraws
  Promised --> Reported: performer reports completion
  Promised --> Closed: performer reneges, or customer cancels
  Reported --> Satisfied: customer declares satisfaction
  Reported --> Promised: customer rejects the report
  Satisfied --> [*]
  Closed --> [*]

Three properties of the model bear directly on Leaf:

  • Finished means nobody is waiting. Winograd (1986): there are "a few states of completion … in which it is mutually recognized that neither party is waiting for further action by the other. All other states represent an incomplete conversation." Leaf's "nobody (still open)" state is an incomplete conversation that no list shows.
  • Only the customer declares satisfaction. The performer reports completion; the customer accepts or rejects that report. Completion is not satisfaction either: a performer who reneges completes the conversation without satisfying it. A later paper by Medina-Mora, Winograd and the Floreses (1992) recast this as four phases, request, agreement, performance and acceptance, always with "an identified customer and a performer".
  • Silence is a move. "Declaring satisfaction may be taken for granted if some amount of time goes by without a declaration to the contrary." After a report, saying nothing counts as accepting it.

The product. The Coordinator was an email client sold by Action Technologies with paid training courses, and was explicitly designed to "coach" its users "to take clearer actions in declaring and following up on their commitments." Each message was sent as a typed act (request, promise, offer, counter-offer, decline, report), and the client tracked every open conversation and its deadline.

Why people rejected it.

  • Field trials went badly. In Carasik and Grantham's trial at Pacific Bell (CHI '88), "anecdotal evidence was far more negative" than the measured scales. The usual explanation is the work of learning it and of labelling every message by hand.
  • Suchman objected to the accounting itself. In "Do categories have politics?" (1994) she argued that sorting speech into these categories is "an agenda of discipline and control" over the people using it, and that the system works as "a technology of accountability", like a bookkeeper's ledger.
  • Winograd conceded where it fails. His reply agreed that "explicit representation of intentions and commitments is more appropriate in some social/organizational situations than others". It had "much greater success" with engineering change orders than among researchers, and "explicit structure is more likely to be seen as an imposition in those cases where organizational activity has been relatively unstructured (e.g., … email)." He still held that the request–promise–report structure is "implicit in all interactions where actions are being coordinated … whether or not they are stated explicitly."

What changed since. Cohen, Carvalho and Mitchell (2004) trained classifiers to tag email as propose, commit or deliver. They noted that the field's difficulties "are avoided if messages can be automatically annotated by intent". Their imagined assistant asks "should I add this new commitment to your todo list?" and "notices outgoing request, may take action if no answer is received promptly". In Leaf that classifier already exists: the agent is a language model that reads every message.

Suchman's objection is the one that survives. The chips that show a split, and Close, Drop and Later, still put commitment categories in front of you. The design answer is to show them only where the default (one message, one request) is wrong. The empirical answer is to watch whether you correct chips or ignore them.

  • "Needs you" comes in two kinds. Answer means a question only you can settle. Review means the agent reports it's done and you check. The "nobody" state is exactly where review went missing.
  • A close that drops owed work must say so. "Done", "withdrawn" and "later" are different outcomes. Resolving over a pending request is a withdrawal you never chose.
  • The reply sets the next state, and it's not an optional flag. Zendesk's "Submit as…" and Gerrit's reply dialog make whoever replies pick whose move is next. Linear and Intercom derive the state from whether the agent's last message asked or answered. --awaits is optional, and nothing notices when it's left off.
  • Split a multi-part question into parts that each settle. No system models "half answered" inside one message. Critique settles each comment separately, and AskUserQuestion answers each question separately.
  • Zero means nothing undecided, not everything finished. In GTD and Inbox Zero, delegated work moves to a trusted "Waiting for" list. Masicampo and Baumeister (2011) found that a concrete plan removes an unfinished goal's pull, but only if the plan is credible.
  • A deferral ends on a condition or a clock. Linear snoozes until a date or new activity, whichever comes first. Superhuman and HEY bring a thread back "if no reply by" a date. Reviewable defers until a new revision.
  • Silence means different things after a report and after a question. After a report, silence can be read as acceptance, labelled "assumed" (Winograd; Zendesk's timer; Intercom Fin's 24 hours). After a question, it is an absence someone has to pursue (conversation analysis).
  • Asking too much ruins the count. Claude Code users approve 93% of permission prompts. Copilot moved to an explicit @copilot mention so people can leave thoughts without triggering work. GitLab built an attention set and cancelled it, because it moved attention without consent.

Five mechanisms tested on the logs; the big surprise is that results land outside their thread

The directions below are assembled from a handful of mechanisms. A second subagent pass tested each mechanism's cheapest falsifier against the grown corpus (59 pages, 177 threads).

MechanismWhat the logs show
A result closes the thread it was promised inIt never did. Of 15 threads resolved after a promise, none got its result posted there. 6 results landed in a version note or a sibling thread, and 9 went to another session by design. A promise is also the thread's last agent word in 24 of 41 cases.
Reading a result acknowledges itPlausible, on thin evidence. Read tracking began on 22 September. The direct evidence is the 6 threads read and then left: 3 were results, where reading settles it, and 3 were a promise and two proposals awaiting a press, where it doesn't.

Another 13 were prose answers, where hiding the Ask is also right, and 1 was wrong. Separately, three times a prose answer ("yes, great on both") got the reply "press the Ask".

About 26% of your messages hold more than one owed thing; that is fewer than the 32% that mix kinds, since a critique and a question often make one request. In a sample of 88 messages weighted toward mixed ones, a third split in more than one defensible way, and for 14 of the 88 (16%) the choice changes what's on your list. The agent already misread two messages this way without loops: it parked a request as "later" (you replied "can we do this change now?"), and it didn't take a prose answer as an answer.
A question survives in proseIt doesn't. Offers such as "tell me and I'll add it" and "say the word and I'll put them up" were never answered, and no agent thread message asked two or more explicit questions. Asks persist: of 19 Asks left after partial answering, 7 were pressed later (median 15 hours), and only 3 were lost with their page.

Two consequences hold whichever direction is picked:

  • A request must be closable from anywhere. Its result can arrive in a version, another thread, or another session.
  • A cross-page list needs aging or a "done with this page". It would start with 58 threads ending on an agent message, 70% of them older than two weeks. 33 of those messages arrived after you last touched their page, so you never saw them.

How these directions were found

Four research subagents covered:

  • review and inbox tools;
  • agent harnesses and workflow engines;
  • theory: speech acts, conversation analysis, GTD and commitment-based management;
  • the real Leaf logs.

Three proposer subagents then worked the same scenarios, each from a different stance: subtract what's there, rebuild around one primitive, or argue against the premise. A and B came from the subtractive stance, C from the rebuild (with A's rules as its default), and D, E and F from the contrarian stance. A last pass tested each mechanism's cheapest falsifier against the logs, and an independent reviewer checked the page against the evidence. The research notes sit beside this page in notes/what-needs-you/research.md.

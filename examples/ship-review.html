What a reader sees

Nothing about the editor changes until the connection drops. Then the save state is replaced by a count of what is waiting, and a line under the document says where those changes are being kept. Click the shot to flip it.

The number counts queued changes, not keystrokes: a paragraph typed straight through is one. Beta readers asked for a number twice in the first week, which is why it says "3 changes waiting" rather than "unsaved".

How it works

Every edit goes to a queue before it goes anywhere else. Online the queue drains as fast as the server answers and nothing about it is visible. Offline it fills, and the local store carries it through a closed tab. 

graph LR
  E[editor] --> Q[change queue]
  Q -->|online| S[server]
  Q -.->|offline| L[local store]
  L -->|reconnect| S

The store is capped at 40 MB a workspace. The largest workspace in the beta reached 31 MB, over six weeks and without reconnecting once, so nothing in the beta hit the cap. What happens at it is the first decision below.

The function after the change, with the three lines the rule turns on marked: 

export function merge(base: Doc, mine: Edit[], theirs: Edit[]): Doc {
  let out = apply(base, theirs);
  for (const edit of mine) {
    const clash = theirs.find((t) => t.field === edit.field);
    if (clash) {
      ask(out, edit, clash);
      continue;
    }
    out = apply(out, [edit]);
  }
  return out;
}
The server's edits land first, always. The reader who was offline is the one being merged in, and that ordering is why every device replaying the same log ends up with the same document. Same field, not same document. Widening this comparison to the whole document is the old rule, and the old rate. The only path that reaches a person. Everything else resolves without a prompt.

The iOS stall reproduces without any of our code

Fifty-four stalls across 2,140 sessions, all on iOS 17.3 and 17.4 and none on 17.2. In every one the queue had already handed the batch to the network layer and no response or error came back, so our own retry never fired. Backgrounding the app and returning releases it, and that is the workaround below. A reproducer that skips our code entirely — the vendor's client posting the same batch — stalls at the same rate, and that reproducer is what the filed report carries.

What a full store does

At 40 MB a workspace, something has to give. Nothing in the beta reached the cap, so this decides what happens to the first workspace that does. My recommendation is the first. Pick one to settle it; if you want a fourth behaviour, the empty option under them takes it in your own words.

effort: donerisk: low Drop the oldest documents
At the cap
the least recently opened document leaves the device
Reader sees
a line naming what left, and how to keep it
Worst case
a document they meant to have offline isn't there

Editing never stops, and that is the whole point of the feature. A document that leaves is still on the server, so what is lost is having it here, not the work in it — and this is the only one of the three that does not punish a reader for not reading a banner. Pinning a document keeps it, and the line that says what left says so.

Nothing is ever dropped, and that is the cleanest sentence any of the three gives a release note. It also fails at exactly the moment offline editing is worth having, and a reader with no connection cannot act on the banner telling them so.

The most respectful of the three on paper. In practice it arrives without warning, mid-sentence, and asks someone who came to write a question about storage — and it still needs the eviction code underneath, for the reader who dismisses it.

How the rollout goes

The beta workspaces already have it. This is about everyone else, and the three shapes differ mostly in how fast the iOS stall reaches people who never agreed to a beta. I recommend the middle one.

Everyone on the day you sign off Ten percent for a week, then everyone An opt-in toggle until the iOS fix lands

The day itself, whichever shape wins:

  1. Turn it on for internal workspaces first thing, and leave it an hour. Every past regression here showed up inside ten minutes.
  2. Point the offline banner at the release note. The help centre page replaces it in the first patch.
  3. Send the announcement below.
  4. Watch queue depth and resync time for the first day; both are on the existing dashboard, and neither carries document content.
  5. Leave the kill switch in for two weeks. It stops new offline sessions and never discards a queue.

The third is a deletion with nothing in its place. I am proposing it because the sentence is a promise we cannot keep: uninstalling the app takes the local store with it, and anything still queued there goes too. It says otherwise in the one place a reader would quote back at support.
